Your positioning framework

The name IS the brand. Marcus Maute owns AI transformation — not a product, not a company, not a topic page. You are the asset. Everything else is a vehicle.

The core doctrine
"Marcus Maute" should become synonymous with AI transformation. Not a separate brand. Not a product. The person compounds. The topic is the current vehicle. If AI transformation gets commoditised in 3 years, your name travels with you. A domain like aitransformation.io does not.
What this means practically
One domain: marcusmaute.com. One name everywhere. The newsletter lives under your name, not as a separate brand. If you ever build a productised thing — course, assessment, advisory programme — give that a distinct name but keep it anchored to you. Never split SEO, backlinks, or subscribers across two identities.
Write like you speak, not like a brand
The site needs you in it — a photo, first-person voice, rough edges only you would write. "I help companies stop doing AI and start using it" beats any third-person positioning statement. Copy should sound like something you would actually say out loud to a CFO over coffee.
What the site still needs — in priority order
1. A real photo of you — in the hero, taking up real estate
2. A "featured in" logo strip for social proof (even aspirational targets)
3. An embedded LinkedIn post to show content velocity
4. Speaking & advisory section with a direct email CTA
5. Newsletter as primary conversion — not a bottom-of-page afterthought
6. Subscriber counts once meaningful (500+ LI followers, 200+ newsletter)
Four signature POVs to own

Pick the ideas you repeat relentlessly. Experts become known for a handful of strongly-held positions, not a broad encyclopaedia.

POV 01
"AI transformation has an infrastructure layer that nobody talks about"
Most conversation is at the application layer — chatbots, copilots. You talk about what sits underneath: data pipelines, inference costs, reliability. This is where you have real authority and where companies actually fail.
POV 02
"The biggest AI failure mode isn't bad models — it's bad organisational design"
Digital transformation failed for the same reason. Connects AI to a proven narrative executives already understand and fear. High shareability. Widely applicable.
POV 03
"Europe is building AI differently — and that's actually a competitive advantage"
Your Zug base gives you a legitimate European lens. Gets you onto EU/DACH media and differentiates you from the US-heavy AI commentariat.
POV 04
"Web3 solved decentralised infra. AI needs to learn from that — and from its mistakes"
Very few people sit at this intersection. Gives you a unique historical comparison that digital transformation writers cannot make. Bridges two audiences and two eras.

LinkedIn post templates

Five plug-and-play formats. Adapt the specifics, keep the structure. Each is engineered for executive audiences.

Template 1 Contrarian Take
Hot take: AI transformation will fail for the same reason digital transformation failed. Not because the technology isn't good enough. Not because the vendors are bad. Because companies are running a 2015 playbook on a 2025 problem. In digital transformation, we hired CDOs, stood up CoEs, ran 90-day sprints, and called it transformation. Then we measured ROI after year one and got confused when there wasn't any. AI transformation is already showing the same symptoms: → AI labs sitting outside the business → POCs that never reach production → "AI strategy" documents that nobody reads → Execs who say "we're investing in AI" but can't name a single changed workflow The companies that got digital transformation right didn't do it by buying more software. They changed how decisions get made. AI transformation is no different. The question isn't "which models are we using?" It's "which decisions are now made differently because of AI — and how do we know?" Still figuring this out myself. Curious what you're seeing on the ground.
Template 2 DT Comparison
In 2012, a McKinsey report declared that "digital transformation" was the defining challenge of the decade. By 2020, 70% of digital transformation initiatives had failed. Sound familiar? We're already saying the same things about AI: — "It's an existential threat if you don't move fast" — "Start with a strategy, not the technology" — "Culture eats AI for breakfast" We've been here before. Here's what actually separated the DT winners from the losers — and what it means for AI: The winners didn't digitise their existing processes. They reimagined them. → For AI: don't automate your current workflows. Question whether those workflows should exist at all. The winners changed their operating model, not just their tech stack. → For AI: if your org chart looks the same after "AI transformation," you haven't transformed. The winners measured differently. → For AI: "number of AI tools deployed" is a vanity metric. "decisions made faster and better" is a real one. The pattern is the same. The speed is 3× higher. You have less time to make the same mistakes.
Template 3 Framework Post
I've been thinking a lot about why AI pilots die in production. After watching this happen across multiple companies, I think it comes down to one thing: The infrastructure layer is an afterthought. Here's what I mean — there are 3 layers to any AI initiative: LAYER 1: The model (This is what everyone obsesses over — GPT-4 vs Claude vs Llama, fine-tuned vs RAG) LAYER 2: The application (The product, the interface, the workflow integration) LAYER 3: The infrastructure (Data pipelines, inference costs, latency, monitoring, failover, cost controls) Most transformation roadmaps are 80% Layer 1, 15% Layer 2, and 5% Layer 3. In production, that ratio flips. The model you chose matters a lot less than whether your data is clean, your pipeline is reliable, and your costs don't blow up at scale. The companies quietly winning at AI transformation aren't always running the fanciest models. They're the ones who invested in the boring layer — and it compounded. What layer does your organisation underinvest in?
Template 4 Story Post
A few months ago, a CFO told me: "We've spent €2M on AI last year and I can't point to a single changed decision." She wasn't anti-AI. She was genuinely frustrated. We spent an hour mapping her company's actual decision-making flows — not the org chart, the real map of who decides what, how fast, based on what information. Turns out: every AI initiative they'd run was optimising for tasks that weren't actually bottlenecks. They'd automated report generation. Nobody had been bottlenecked by report generation. They'd built a "smart" contract review tool. Legal reviewed 12 contracts a month. That wasn't their bottleneck either. What actually slowed them down? Pricing decisions that took 3 weeks and required 4 layers of approval. One AI initiative, properly targeted, would have paid for the €2M in a quarter. AI transformation isn't about deploying AI everywhere. It's about finding the 2–3 places where better, faster decisions create outsized value — and starting there. The €2M isn't the problem. The target selection is.
Template 5 Hot Take / Org Design
Unpopular opinion: your "Head of AI" hire might be slowing down your AI transformation. Hear me out. When you create a Head of AI role and put all AI initiatives under one function, you've accidentally built a bottleneck. Now every AI idea has to go through the AI team. The AI team gets protective of its projects. The business units wait to be "served" AI rather than building it into their own workflows. This is exactly what happened with digital transformation. We created CDOs. Then everyone waited for the CDO to do digital. The companies further along with AI transformation don't have an AI department. They have AI-native teams — where the product manager, the analyst, the ops lead are all expected to know how to deploy AI in their domain. The Head of AI should be killing their own job description. Not by doing more AI. By making AI so embedded in every team that nobody needs a centralised AI function anymore. Agree? Disagree? Would love to hear what you're actually seeing.
